Sombun buys a ticket for the first time since he moved to Bangkok. He stands behind a long line, waiting for the next train to Asok. He taps his feet as he waits for the BTS sky train, instead of his usual motorcycle taxi. Below the tracks supported by concrete pillars is a complex system of roads and canals, shared by cars, trucks, buses, scooters, tuk-tuks, and passenger boats.

Ploy gets onto the motorcycle sidesaddle like her mother taught her. Her hands grab onto the motorcycle taxi driver, known in Thailand as “Win Motosai.” The “Win” speeds off — his helmet left behind on the bench. He took Ploy to her destination the fastest way, not necessarily the safest, and legality was out the window since he abandoned his helmet. He rode past the police booth, going against traffic, without a care.

A Win blows past Saeng as he snores in his police booth. He was halfway through chowing down his second stick of fishballs before he fell asleep. His wallet contains mostly cash from bribes he scared out of people in the morning. Most of it won’t stay in his hand. His boss gets an eighty per cent cut, and his boss’s boss probably gets a cut of that. When he joined the force, he didn’t expect to be corrupt. Only years of denial and telling himself “It’s okay, everybody does it” have let him reconcile with his actions.

Nok stares down at his wallet after he gets to the hospital. There is a thousand-baht note missing since he was in a rush and gave some cop the note because he couldn’t miss his doctor’s appointment. His family has been waiting for the doctor’s results all month. Nok is cancer-free. He calls his mom as soon as the doctor finishes speaking.

Bua just found out her son is cancer-free. She immediately buys 5,000 boiled eggs to donate to the temple, along with whatever sacred thing she believed had granted her wish. By sunset, monks, samaneras, and other parishioners distribute these eggs to labourers, the needy, neighbouring elders, and stray dogs. Nobody knows exactly why there are so many eggs that day; they just know that someone somewhere had a reason to be grateful.

“Luang Pho! Luang Pho Pom!” calls a young monk, running up the stairs of the pavilion. He finds Luang Pho Pom sitting in meditation. Luang Pho Pom opens his eyes to a rumble of the diesel engine and a truck backing toward the charity storehouse for dry foods to be donated to the needy. The driver steps out of the truck, greets the monk by pressing his two hands into a wai, and begins unloading a little short of two hundred crates of boiled eggs.

Mad wipes sweat off his forehead with a small cotton towel draped over his neck. He stopped to look around the temple just for a moment after finishing his third delivery today. It had been years since he was inside a temple’s walls. Mad, short for Ahmad, is a Muslim from Ranong. Realising there was little outside the ports, he left with three thousand baht and his father’s pickup truck. The truck is long gone now.
