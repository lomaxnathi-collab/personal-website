Meditation

November 2025

๏ It was the Monday after. Above the blackboard, where hung a portrait of the King, only a pale rectangle on the blue-green wall remained next to the image of his wife, the now Queen-mother. All of us had a black ribbon on the left sleeve of our school uniform. It was sunny, yet we didn’t play in the fields. The food didn’t have a lot of flavour. We tried not to think about it, but we couldn’t. Dismal — that’s the word. We were dismal.

None of us knew what it was like to experience a succession, not my mum, nor my teachers. It was 1946 when Thailand last mourned for her king. Far too long ago for anyone to remember the emotion. King Bhumibol Adulyadej (ภูมิพลอดุลยเดช), or as he is known in the West, Rama IX, reigned for over seventy years. He wasn’t just a monarch but an ever-present figure in all our lives. To us, the King is the foundation of our society. Everything begins and ends with the Monarchy. Why does it, one may ask; why does this single institution matter so much? The real answer is: I don’t know. And I shouldn’t attempt to answer this question. But I shall speak what I do know: my family and I, we love our King. There is no way that I can put it better. We are thankful, eternally.

In second grade, my teacher taught me a lesson I will never forget. “We Thai people do not live through decades or centuries. We live through reigns.” It sounded like poetic abstract things the adults say to make history grander than it actually is, I thought. To me, it didn’t particularly make sense until the King’s death. Only then did the saying return with its full weight. An absence such as this hasn’t been felt since my great-grandparents’ time. The monarchy in our lives is a measure of time. This concept is best expressed in the Thai bestseller, Four Reigns, by former Prime Minister Mom Rajawongse Kukrit Pramoj.

The book begins in the last years of the 19th century during the reign of Rama V or King Chulalongkorn (จุฬาลงกรณ์). Mae Ploy, the protagonist, is a young girl raised within the royal court. Through her perspective, we see national events contextualised by each reign. To her, the King is not abstract or aloof. He is a man. She learns loyalty not by force or by command, but by love. After Rama V passed away suddenly, Prince Vajiravudh (วชิราวุธ), his son, the future Rama VI, remarked, “The last position I would like to attain is King, for I must wait until my own father passes.” In that single sentence lies the depth of filial devotion that defines the monarchy itself.

As she matures, Mae Ploy falls in love and marries her husband, Khun Prem, a civil servant, and leaves the palace to start a new life outside its walls. The story then follows her through the reign of Rama VI, who experimented with the idea of a constitution, and Rama VII, King Prajadhipok (ประชาธิปก), who prepared the country for democracy as the last absolute monarch of Siam until the bloodless revolution of 1932. Indeed, “democracy” came to Siam, and “autocracy” became the past. The revolutionaries, however, held onto power; their undemocratic ways eventually forced Rama VII to abdicate. In his abdication statement, he said: “I am willing to surrender the powers I formerly exercised to the people as a whole, but I am not willing to turn them over to any individual or any group to use in an autocratic manner without heeding the voice of the people.” The constitutional power of the monarch lessened, yet his loyalty and love for us only nurtured ours for him.

When I first learned the details of King Prajadhipok’s abdication, I remember feeling a kind of sadness, not for the loss of power, but for what it revealed about love and responsibility for his citizens. His words didn’t sound political to me; they sounded human. It reminded me that kingship, at its best, isn’t about control, but about faith in the people. Even as the throne’s power diminished, his love for the nation seemed to grow larger, as if, stripped of authority, all that remained was mere sincerity.

Four Reigns then leads us to the fourth reign of the book, the reign of Rama VIII, King Ananda Mahidol (อานันทมหิดล), a nine-year-old who came to power in a time of turmoil. For most of his childhood, he lived abroad, staying away from the volatile political situation in Siam. When he was thirteen, he visited his kingdom for the first time as its monarch. Despite his youth and absence, being the only stable power figure in Siam amid political crises, he was deeply revered. A story about him passed down in my family mentions that before returning to Switzerland to continue his studies, the young king gave a radio address to the Thai people, promising that he would study hard so that he might return one day as a good king. The speech was heartfelt and innocent, made by a boy to his subjects, reminiscent of the numerous times we all have promised our parents that we would do well in school so we may take care of them in the future.

When I first heard about this radio address, I was twelve, almost as old as he had been, thirteen. I remember thinking how strange it was that a boy, not much older than me, could already be called a king. I imagined him, about to board a ship back to Switzerland, trying to sound brave into a microphone that carried his voice across the nation. In some way, I felt like I knew him. We were both just boys trying to do our best for the people who loved us.

After the King left Siam, the whole world ultimately fell into war. As Japan attempted to conquer all of East Asia, bombing and massacring everything and everyone in its path, Germany rapidly conquered most of Europe, surrounding the king of Siam in the European hinterlands. King Ananda Mahidol continued his studies in law and returned to Thailand after the war ended. Twenty years old and eager to put his studies to use, Rama VIII sought to improve his country as he promised. My great-grandfather Kew, a son of a Chinese immigrant living in Bang Rak, Bangkok, told and retold until his death a story of the time the King personally went to Soi Sampeng, a Chinatown in Bangkok, to resolve disputes between the Thai and Chinese populations. How my great-grandfather described the visit sounded miraculous. The ethnic Thai and Chinese tension disappeared instantaneously as if the King’s presence had a magical impact.

My great-grandfather’s story, retold by my great aunt, Yai Le, about that visit to Sampeng, stayed with me. I used to imagine the scene the way it was described, the market alive with vendors shouting in Thai, Cantonese, Teochew, Hainanese, Mandarin, Malay, the smell of frying oil, the street only a few yards wide and then, suddenly, silence when the King appeared. What kind of presence could quiet a whole street, I wondered. It wasn’t fear but respect, shared by everybody. He wasn’t there to rule, but to listen to everyone about their lives, their hardships. Growing up, I heard so many stories like that, stories where the King wasn’t withdrawn, always walking among his people. The king wasn’t so much a figure in the portraits inside government offices but a part of my family history. I grew up loving the monarchy long before I could explain why, and it took me years to understand for myself what devotion is.

In Pramoj’s Four Reigns, Mae Ploy’s health slowly deteriorates as she ages. She grows older and more frail, foreshadowing her near and inevitable death. Meanwhile, the young king is ready to honour his promise: to reign for the benefit of the Thai people. On the afternoon of 9 June 1946, just days before Rama VIII is expected to return to Europe to finish his doctoral law degree, Mae Ploy, bedridden, hears the news, and her heart melts away. Siam’s young king, Ananda Mahidol, shot dead in his bedroom, a bullet to the forehead. Thailand, still recovering from the war, whether that be the damage of the bombs dropped upon Bangkok or the economic recession that followed, loses its monarch, the one single establishment it could rely on to be there without fail. So ends the novel, with the death of an old woman and a young king.

From this moment of grief comes a new reign, seventy years long. King Bhumibol Adulyadej, Rama IX, was crowned king of Thailand, fresh out of an absolute monarchy and entering Cold War politics. Travelling tirelessly across the nation, visiting villages, sponsoring development projects, and listening to the poor, he transformed the meaning of kingship into one of service. He transformed the throne from a symbol of power to meaningful humility and duty, of his loyalty to us.

Though I wasn’t born until far later in his reign, the image of the King in my memory wasn’t of him in his golden coronation robe sitting on a throne, but in mud-caked shoes with a camera hanging around his neck, traversing rough terrain in the mountains or the jungles. A teacher I had in the third grade told me about when the King visited a small village far out in the woods back in the 60s. With little to no contact with Bangkok, the villagers were minding their own business when a dusty jeep pulled up, and out stepped this man in a denim shirt, pants rolled up, shoes absolutely covered in mud. The King walked up the wooden ladder steps in front of the village leader’s house; the ladder creaked, mud stains left on each step. The village headman came out, gave him a small wai, a Thai greeting gesture of placing palms together and bowing, like “sawasdee krub, governor.” He thought the man was some district official who came to inspect chickens or something. Nothing near the formality a king deserves. The King took off his muddy shoes, asked the headman about the village’s wellbeing, then sat cross-legged on the floor, and had dinner with his family: grilled fish, sticky rice, and chilli paste. The headman, trying to be a good host, even patted the King on the shoulder and said something like, “Eat more, Mr Governor, you’re too skinny!” After dinner, he saw the King out to his jeep.

As the leader turned around to go back to his house, the King asked the man, “Do you know who I am?”

“No, sir,” the man responded.

The King then said, “My name is Bhumibol, Bhumibol Adulyadej.”

The poor man froze. Then he dropped to his knees so fast the whole house shook. He started apologising, probably seeing his whole life flash before his eyes. “I touched His Majesty’s shoulder! I’m done! My head’s gone!”

The King then let out a little giggle, asking the man to get up.

That is how we Thai people remember him, a king who can eat sticky rice on the floor and get his feet muddy. He is a man, not a god.

He continued to travel all across Thailand to the farthest corners, from rice paddies to mangrove forests, from mountains to little islands that surveyors forget in the maps. You name it, he went there. Sometimes by jeep, sometimes by helicopter, sometimes by a tractor with a royal seal taped to the side. People used to joke that if you saw a man taking notes in the middle of nowhere, it’d either be a local inspector or the King himself.

He’d show up to far-flung villages, ask about a drought, and a villager, not recognising him, would say, “Terrible, it’s so dry, the government doesn’t listen to us at all.” The King would just nod and take notes, and a few months later, the water would start flowing, and the people would say, “The government really listened.” He wasn’t the man who showed up in a limo wearing European suits, but the man who showed up when no one else did.

The government situation remained unstable. Parliament opened and closed — democracy for a year or two, then a coup d’état. By the early 1970s, the tension finally burst. On October 14, 1973, students flooded Bangkok’s Ratchadamnoen Avenue, demanding a genuine constitution, one that truly represents the people and not the Western-educated elite at the top and the military. For days, Bangkok was filled with chants, flags, and the stench of sweat. When violence erupted, it was the King who intervened, opening the palace gates to shelter the protesters. For a short moment, it seemed like true change was coming. The military junta was overthrown, the premier fled the country, a two-thousand-person group was appointed by the King to draft a new constitution. Thailand seemed to be moving toward progress and peace.

The following years were uneasy. Radios buzzed with propaganda, newspapers were filled with half-truths, and rumours of communists crept through tea shops and classrooms. In the cafes, old men argued over which side truly loved the nation, sipping their coffee sweetened by condensed milk. The royal portraits in homes gathered dust as families focused on surviving. Vietnam fell to communism. The Khmer Rouge began its reign of terror. The communist overthrow of the Lao Royal Family in 1975 shook the Thai monarchy to its core. Within a couple of years, the ideals of 1973 lost their meaning, once fragrant, now dry and fragile in their memory.

Over the course of 1976, the political situation started slowly falling apart again. The exiled premier returned, and union leaders were lynched in the name of anti-communism. In response, students at Thammasat University made plays reenacting the lynchings, using art to mirror truth, hoping the country might see itself clearly again. But the plays were twisted by rumour and fear. Soon, pamphlets accused the students of mocking the monarchy, of plotting against the nation itself. The protests spilt beyond the campus gates.

Then came October 6, 1976. Dawn broke over Phra Nakhon, the centre of Bangkok, with the sound of gunfire. Students at Thammasat sought refuge behind classroom walls as the police advanced, their words overwhelmed by the crack of rifles. The smoke drifted through the old city, mixing with the sweetness of morning incense from nearby temples and the gasoline odour from the ferries crossing the Chao Phraya River. The words turned into cries. My aunt Nong, or as I call her, Pa Nong, was thirteen then, living with a courtier’s family not far from the university, next to Tong Sanam Luang, a large lawn the shape of a Harkness table, lined with trees. She said, “When the first mortar shell landed, the windows rattled like my teeth when I’m shivering cold.” Outside, the air was thick with dust. Monks fled barefoot through the alleys. The bells at the nearby temple, Wat Mahathat, clanged, even though no one was ringing them. As morning became afternoon, some students were injured, some were dead, some were dragged out and hanged from trees in Tong Sanam Luang, people beating their corpses with sticks, folding chairs, and whatever they could find.

When evening came, the curfew clock struck seven-thirty, and the city fell into an eerie stillness. No one dared to speak above a whisper. Somewhere, a loudspeaker played the national anthem, but no one sang along. Pa Nong said she could hear only the ticking of the clock and the faraway hum of army trucks. That night, Phra Nakhon felt hollow, emptied out of all its colour and sound. The next morning, the newspapers printed in bold black letters: “ไทยฆ่าไทย ธรรมศาสตร์นองเลือด;” “Thai kill[ed] Thai, Bloodshed [at] Thammasat.” Even the stray dogs were quiet that day. The city had forgotten how to breathe.

Fast forward thirty-something years, Nathi Pannathammaruksa (นที ปัณณธรรมรักษ์) was born. The city had changed. It learned how to breathe again. The bullet holes were gone. The palace walls were whitewashed. Sky trains and subways dashed through the city faster than before. Old wooden houses in Phra Nakhon became concrete storefronts. The streets soldiers marched on are lined by vendors selling Moo Ping and sticky rice or Thai iced coffee. Pa Nong would sometimes point toward Thammasat as we drove past and say, “ตรงนั้นนะ ตอนนั้นปืนดังทั้งวันเลย;” “Right there, the guns went off all day.” I never quite knew what to say, so I just nodded, eating my grilled pork in silence, wondering how a city could hold so much memory under so much noise.

Despite all the changes, we still had a constant. The tanks were gone from the streets, but the King was still ever-present. He was on the calendars, in the classrooms, on taxi dashboards right above the meter. My earliest memory of him wasn’t any special occasion but a small, hand-sized photograph in the drawing room: he was in a barrister’s robe preparing to hand out diplomas to law graduates; he stood straight, serious, and slightly tired. To me, he wasn’t a distant monarch, nor did he seem like one; if anything, he seemed like a professor who had graded too many papers and nonetheless showed up smiling the next morning.

As I grew up, I began noticing him in more places, not just in photographs and television broadcasts. I would also see him on the crisp 20-baht note shopkeepers hold with two hands, on the stamps my mother used for New Year’s cards, on coins I flipped to see who would do the kick-off in a soccer game. Even the morning sun seemed to fall differently on the yellow shirts people liked to wear on Mondays, his birthday colour. But even as a child, a part of me wondered what it meant to love someone I would never meet, and whether that love was mine or something I inherited without question. Nevertheless, I know what I felt was real.

The King was everywhere but never intrusive. When I started playing the saxophone, my teacher handed me a small booklet and said, “You should learn this: ‘Love at Sundown.’ The King wrote it.” At first, I thought he was joking. “Kings didn’t write jazz; they issued decrees and waved from balconies,” I thought. But there I was, squeaking through his melodies in the humid Bangkok afternoons, right after I learned my scales. The tunes were beautiful. They were the kinds of tunes that soothed you and made you feel like you were bathing in candlelight. Somewhere between wrong notes and breathless bars, I began to understand that this was another way he was reaching us, through creating something beautiful and sharing it with everyone.

Eventually, I realised that everyone I loved had their own version of the King. My great aunt’s King was the young one who handed her her diploma; Pa Nong’s King was the one who opened the palace gates during the protests; mine was the one teaching his son how to play saxophone. I never met him, of course, but he was always there for me, for all of us Thais.

On 13 October 2016, I came home from soccer practice after school to find the television already on. The most recent news about the King’s health was positive. He had recovered from his fever, but his kidney condition was “not yet stable.” Crowds of loyal well-wishers gathered around Siriraj Hospital, where the King was being treated, and the Grand Palace, wearing pink, symbolising hope, offering prayers and support. Rumours of his severely deteriorating condition were spreading like wildfire on Facebook tabloids. My mother received phone calls from other relatives as if the King were a close family member we shared. The television we were watching switched to black, replacing the regular programming. I dropped my bag off by the door and sat next to my mother. Her hands were clasped as she stared at the royal seal in black and white. Then came the word we knew but hoped we’d never hear used for a current monarch, “สวรรคต;” “Died.” I began tearing up even before I heard the word. I looked at my mother, tears rolling down her face. She began weeping. Sure, I’d seen teardrops here and there, but never her weeping, not when we were tight on money, not even when she lost a friend from college. I inched closer to her, not saying a word, and we held each other tight.

That night, the city became silent. Street vendors that usually stay till midnight went home early. The cars drove more slowly. The next Monday at school, the portrait above the blackboard was gone, only a pale rectangle on the blue-green wall was left. We all wore black ribbons on our sleeves. The sky was clear, too clear. Everything was different. After my father died, the King also became the reassuring male presence I oriented myself towards. Only then, after his death, did I realise that he had become the closest thing I could confidently call a father.

Mom Rajawongse Kukrit Pramoj once said in a BBC interview, “[the] institution of the monarchy is the soul of the Thai nation.” Like other grand statements about the King, I had a hard time fully comprehending what that meant when I first heard it until I started noticing things: thousands lining up with flowers waiting to lay them at the palace gates, strangers helping each other cross the street, giving free food and water to everyone there. We’re all united together as Thais. I still don’t have a perfect answer for what binds me to this institution — whether it is history, longing, or something tender I cannot name. This has nothing to do with politics or power struggles. This is about a feeling that runs so deep it couldn’t be described perfectly. This is only a young boy’s attempt. ๛
