Three Lessons from Plutarch

October 2026

Know then thyself, presume not God to scan;
The proper study of mankind is man.

— Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man

Marcus Tullius Cicero and Marcus Antonius, commonly referred to in English as Mark Antony, were two of the famed Romans we still hear about today, and they hated each other with a fiery vitriol, so much so that one of them ended up putting the other’s severed head and hands on display above the speaker’s platform in the Roman Forum. Plutarch’s biographies of the two men are littered with similar dramatics. Still, with close analysis, they are truly about rather ordinary issues: how much to care about our reputation, how far to let people we love steer us, and what exactly to do when the rules seem to stand in the way of doing the right thing.

I’m certain you understand the general contours. Cicero was a great lawyer and orator, a self-made man from a small town so talented he rose to the top of Roman society and politics that the word Ciceronian exists, and the one who spent the last year of his life attacking Antony in a series of blistering orations. Antony was Julius Cæsar’s number-two, a brilliant soldier with appetites a bit too big for him, who outlived Cicero by about a dozen years before dying in Egypt in the arms of Cleopatra. Between the two men, their lives cover the last decades of the Roman Republic, a time when the centuries-old system of elected magistrates and senators collapsed into civil wars and, ultimately, an Autocracy.

Plutarch, a Greek writing about 150 years later, was not mainly trying to tell the story from a political lens. He wrote about lives, not history, sharing small details, whether they be fantastical, humorous, or perhaps ancient references, which often reveal more about a person’s character than battles and wars. He wanted his readers to look at these characters as one would look into a mirror and reflect. That includes the bad examples. Antony, for example, was there as a warning.

To read the two Lives the way Plutarch wanted them read is to read them as a source of questions about how to live. Three questions returned repeatedly. Each is one on which reasonable people disagree, and on each question Plutarch had something worth reading, even where he may be disagreeable.

1. How much should we care what other people think of us?

The name “Cicero” comes from the Latin cicer, a chickpea. Supposedly, one of his ancestors had a dent in his nose that resembled one. When Cicero entered politics, his friends encouraged him to abandon the rather silly name. He refused, declaring that he would make the “Cicero” name more glorious than any old gens, more than the Scauri and the Catuli (Cic.1). On the one hand, it is a refusal towards rejecting his roots. On the other, a message he sends by making such a declaration is that he will win the world’s admiration on his own terms. From the very first chapter, Cicero was a man fueled by his thirst for recognition.

Plutarch then gives us a glimpse of the moral of the story. As a young man, Cicero asked the Delphic oracle how he could become famous. The Priestess told him to make “his own nature, and not the opinion of the multitude, his guide in life” (Cic. 5). For a time he took the advice. He kept a low profile, studied philosophy and rhetoric, and put up with being called “the Greek” and “the Scholar,” terms analogous to the modern words: “nerd” or “dork.” Then his ambition got the better of him, and he tossed himself into courts of law and politics.

The chapter right after captures Plutarch’s view best. Cicero served as quæstor, a junior official, in Sicily. He did a good job and was well liked. He came home to Rome sure that he was the talk of the town. On the way, he ran into a prominent man he took to be a friend, and asked him what the Romans had been saying of his achievements. The friend replied: “Where, pray, have you been, Cicero, all this while?” (Cic.6). Cicero was disheartened. Plutarch described what modern psychologists call the “spotlight effect.”

But Plutarch does not stop with a laugh. The lesson cut Cicero’s ambition down for a moment, but his delight in praise and his desire for glory stayed with him “until the very end, and very often confounded his saner reasonings” (Cic. 6). The remainder of the biography corroborated so. After he exposed the Catilinarian conspiracy, the pinnacle of his career, Cicero repeatedly championed this success. He made himself despised not because of any wicked deeds, but because of his constant self-praise; no assembly could meet without hearing about Catiline once again, and he filled his writings with his own laudations (Cic. 24). In the comparison of Cicero with the Hellenic orator Demosthenes, Plutarch singles out his pride as Cicero’s characteristic flaw.

In Plutarch’s telling, caring about reputation isn’t merely vain and tacky. It warps decisions that matter. When Cicero had to decide how to punish the captured conspirators, a reason he leaned towards the harshest penalty was the fear of being thought of as a coward, especially since he did not have much reputation for courage (Cic. 19). Afterwards, driven out of Rome, and despite his philosophical pretensions, Cicero fell into despair, “looking off towards Italy like a disconsolate lover,” and displayed a pettiness of spirit that belied his education (Cic. 32). Public opinion has the power to “wash away reason, like a dye, from the soul of man” (Cic. 32). Cicero had become so dependent on the opinion of the multitude that, once their approval was withdrawn, he was left without any firm foundation for his own sense of worth.

It would be perhaps too simple to conclude that Plutarch simply wants us to stop caring what people think. But Antony, the other half of this bitter duo, shows the opposite failure. Antony cared unbelievably little about dignity or respect. As Cæsar’s right-hand-man, he partied openly. After drinking all night at the wedding of a comic actor, he was summoned to the Forum in the morning and vomited into his toga before the assembly (Ant. 9). The respectable classes came to loathe him, and his behaviour made Cæsar’s regime itself less popular. Not caring what anybody thinks, it appears, is not the same as following one’s own nature. Antony was guided by his hedonism, seeking to satisfy his appetites and ignoring disapproval.

So where does Plutarch leave us exactly? Plutarch’s position is more subtle than just “ignore public opinion.” The oracle did not tell Cicero to disregard fame or glory; she told him how to get it, by following his own nature. Fame is supposed to follow from becoming the person you are, not from its replacement. One could reasonably argue that Cicero’s hunger was what drove him to his greatness, and they’d be correct. Cicero, for all his vanity, was free of envy and lavish in praising others, calling Aristotle “a river of liquid gold” (Cic. 24). The answer isn’t to stop caring entirely. Antony isn’t much of a role model. We must care about the right people’s opinions for the right reasons, and try to keep it from becoming our guide in life. The tragedy of Cicero was not his desire for admiration; it was his asphyxiation without such.

2. How much should we let the people closest to us steer our choices?

If the Life of Cicero is about a man dominated by other people’s opinions, the Life of Antony is about a man dominated by other people. Antony, as Plutarch introduced him to the reader, was a gifted, handsome young man who was led astray by others. His friend Curio dragged him into drunkenness, womanising, and excessive spending, leaving him with a two-hundred-fifty talent debt, which was excessive for his years. The friendship “fell upon him like a pest” (Ant. 2). The pattern repeats again with all those he found important. Of his wife, Fulvia, a political provocatrix, Plutarch writes that she was not interested in housekeeping, but “wanted to rule a ruler, to command a commander.” Cleopatra, he then notes dryly, was much indebted to Fulvia for teaching Antony “to endure a woman’s sway” (Ant. 10).

When Antony took charge of the eastern half of the Roman world, flatterers crowded around him. He, Plutarch says, had a kind of naïveté: he was slow to notice his faults, but when he did he was sorry and made amends. He also enjoyed being mocked, so he had trouble realising those who criticised him in jest could also flatter him in earnest. The flatterers exploited this by mixing bold speech with their flattery “like a piquant sauce,” so that their praise seemed honest too (Ant.24). This dynamic is forever familiar. The best yes-men are those who seem to be the only people willing to tell the honest truth.

Then enters the scene the “crowning evil” in Antony’s life, the one that roused passions “still hidden and quiescent in him,” Cleopatra (Ant. 25). The details of her influence are familiar to many: the golden barge on the Cydnus, the conversation more captivating than her beauty, the winter in Alexandria where the two squandered what the Greek orator Antiphon called "the most costly outlay, namely, time" (Ant. 26–28). As Antony approached Syria in preparation for his campaign against Parthia, the passion that had seemed subdued erupted anew, prompting him to summon Cleopatra. Plutarch even invoked the Platonic image of the “stubborn and unmanageable beast of the soul.” Antony was so eager to spend the winter with her that he started the campaign too early and ran it confusedly, to be generous, “as if he were under the influence of certain drugs or of magic rites” (Ant. 37).

The climax is the naval battle at Actium in 31 BC, where Antony’s fleet faced Octavian’s. Antony’s army was far superior to his navy, which was so lacking in crew that his captains rounded up passers-by, mule-drivers, and farmhands from Greece to row the ships. At this point, “to such an extent, now, was Antony an appendage of the woman" that he chose to fight at sea "to please Cleopatra" (Ant. 62). His general Canidius urged him to send Cleopatra away and fight on land, where he was most experienced, but Cleopatra prevailed (Ant. 63).

When her sixty ships hoisted sail and fled in the midst of the battle, Antony followed. Antony made it clear as day that he was governed “neither by the sentiments of a commander nor of a brave man, nor even by his own,” but proved the joke that “the soul of the lover dwells in another's body": he "betrayed and ran away from those who were fighting and dying in his cause" (Ant.66). Afterward he sat alone at the prow of her ship for three days, holding his head in his hands, unable to face her (Ant. 67). His soldiers, meanwhile, could not believe he had deserted them, and held together for a week before they gave up (Ant.68).

Cicero doesn’t exactly avoid this either. While Cicero was hesitating over the conspirators’ fate, his wife Terentia came to him with the news of a favourable omen from a ceremony and urged him on. She was neither gentle nor timid, and took more part in Cicero’s political concerns than in her household ones (Cic. 20). Later, Plutarch claims, it was Terentia’s jealousy of the sister of Clodius, a politician, that drove Cicero to testify against him in a scandal trial, which made Clodius his mortal enemy and led directly to his exile (Cic. 29–31). And at the end of his life, he let himself be charmed by the young Octavian, Cæsar’s nineteen-year-old heir, who even went so far as to call him “father.” What drew Cicero to the young man, besides his loathing for Antony, was "his natural craving for honour," and Brutus, furious, wrote that Cicero was not winning liberty for his country but "wooing a kind master for himself" (Cic. 45). Cicero threw his prestige behind the young man against Antony. Octavian promptly made a deal with Antony and handed Cicero over to be killed. Plutarch's verdict: "Here, indeed, more than at any other time, Cicero was led on and cheated, an old man by a young man" (Cic. 46).

On the surface, Plutarch’s message seems old-fashioned and frankly sexist: strong women ruin men. Fulvia, Cleopatra, and Terentia are all cast as shadows working behind their husbands, and Cleopatra is described in a manner akin to disease or witchcraft. But if one looks closely, one would see a more valuable lesson. Cleopatra does not create anything in Antony; she wakes passions that were already there (Ant. 25). The flatterers succeed because of Antony's naïve character. Octavian succeeded because Cicero was beyond desperate to be the elder statesman, guiding a promising young leader. Being influenced is not the problem itself. After all, Antony’s second wife, Octavia, sister to Octavian, influenced him too. She brokered peace between the two men, and even after her husband left her for Cleopatra and after his death, she went on raising all his children, even ones not her own (Ant. 35, 54, 87). The question is not whether we are steered, but by whom, and whether we can discern the difference.

In truth, we should let the people we love influence us, and in fact, as little as one may like to admit, we cannot help it. We, however, owe it to them and to ourselves to keep our own judgment in working order. The insight is psychological. The people who can lead us astray are those who speak to some underlying desires we have: Antony’s appetite or Cicero’s need to be admired in these cases. Therefore, to know one’s Achilles’ heel is to know where to look for the people who will exploit it.

3. Is it ever right to break the rules to save a free society?

In 63 BC, the year he was consul, Cicero uncovered a conspiracy led by a bankrupt aristocrat named Catiline. Catiline had an army gathering in the Italian countryside hills, and his allies inside Rome were planning to set fires around the city and murder many leaders. Cicero got hold of written proof, letters the plotters had foolishly given to Gallic envoys, and arrested five of the ringleaders, including Lentulus, a prætor, that very year (Cic. 18-19).

Here lies the problem. These men were Roman citizens, nobles at that, and citizens had a right not to be put to death without an appeal to the people. Cicero was, to say the least, torn. He was sure that if the men suffered "any milder penalty than death" they would break out again, and, as we saw, he feared being thought a coward (Cic. 19). He laid this matter before the Senate. Silanus proposed that the men be taken to prison “and there suffer extremest punishment” (Cic.20). Then Julius Cæsar, still a rising politician, proposed instead that they be stripped of their property and held in chains in Italian towns until Catiline was defeated. The proposal was merciful. Cicero's own friends swung behind it, reasoning that he would face less criticism if no one was executed, and even Silanus backtracked, claiming that by “extremest punishment” he had only meant prison (Cic. 21). It was Catulus and then Cato, the Republic's sternest moralist, who steered the Senate back to capital punishment.

Cicero carried out the sentence himself. He led Lentulus through the Forum to prison and handed him to the executioner, then repeated the same action with every other co-conspirator. Seeing each of their friends in the Forum, hoping the men would be rescued, he called out to them, “They have lived” (vixerunt), the Roman way of avoiding the ill-omened words “they are dead” (Cic. 22). The moment, in Plutarch’s eyes, was a triumph. Cicero had quenched the greatest of revolutions “with the fewest possible evils, without sedition and commotion” (Cic. 22).

The executions became the central fact of Cicero's political life, the achievement he would not stop boasting about (back to question one) and the weapon his enemies used against him. A few years later, his enemy Clodius, now a tribune, denounced him for putting citizens to death without trial, and Cæsar told the people that he did not think it "right or lawful" to execute men that way. Cicero fled Rome, Clodius had a vote of banishment passed against him, and his house was burned to the ground (Cic. 30–33). The rule Cicero had set aside in the name of saving the Republic came back to destroy him.

In 43 BC, Antony, Octavian, and a third general named Lepidus formed an alliance called the Triumvirate and drew up a list of their enemies, the "proscriptions": anyone named on it could be killed, and his property seized. The three men met for three days on a small island in a river near Bononia, where they divided up the Roman world as they knew it “as though it were an ancestral inheritance” and then haggled over whom to murder. Octavian gave up Cicero, whom he had called father; Antony gave up his own uncle, Lucius Cæsar; Lepidus gave up his brother. "Nothing, in my opinion," Plutarch writes, "could be more savage or cruel than this exchange" (Ant. 19). When Cicero's head and hand were brought to Antony, he gazed at them, "laughing aloud for joy many times," and had them displayed on the Rostra, the speaker's platform from which Cicero had attacked him in the Philippics (Ant. 20). In the Life of Cicero, Plutarch adds that the Romans shuddered, "for they thought they saw there, not the face of Cicero, but an image of the soul of Antony" (Cic.49).

Plutarch himself never explicitly made the claim that the execution of the Catilinarians led to the proscriptions, and it would be unfair to equate them. Cicero acted against real, armed conspirators, with the Senate’s approval, in an existential crisis of the state; Antony and his partners were settling scores. But by comparing these two acts, the implication is clear. Once killing without trial is justified by necessity, the question becomes who gets to decide what is necessary.

Every modern democracy has had to decide how much the rules can bend in a crisis: Lincoln suspending habeas corpus during the Civil War, governments detaining suspected terrorists without trial, leaders declaring emergencies to get around legislatures. The case for bending is serious, and Cato made it in the Roman Senate: a republic that follows procedure while armed enemies burn it down will not have any procedures left. If Cicero had kept the conspirators alive and they had been freed by a mob, history might remember him as a fool rather than a hero, and Plutarch suggests that this fear was not imaginary. The point of rules like the right to a trial is that they are made in calm times to bind us in frightened ones. Plutarch shows that Cicero's decision was shaped not just by danger but by his fear of seeming cowardly and by his wife's urging (Cic.19–20), exactly the kind of pressures that procedures are supposed to protect against. The emergency that seems to justify the exception is almost always one in which the person making the exception is least able to judge it clearly. The Catilinarian executions may well have been necessary in the short term. But they taught Rome that citizens could be killed without trial for the good of the state, and Plutarch lets us watch that lesson being learned by worse men than Cicero.

The Conclusion

Looking over the three main questions, one couldn’t help but notice that they are the same question asked in three ways. Plutarch asked questions about what happens when something external takes the place of an individual’s judgement. Cicero and Antony were both extraordinary and talented men, and both were undone less by enemies than by when and where they let something else do their thinking, whether that be public opinion, the voice of a lover or flatterer, or the pressure of a crisis. That is exactly the kind of lesson Plutarch meant his Lives to teach, and it is why he cares so much about small moments rather than the grand sweep of events.

It is also worth saying that Plutarch is a fair judge. He calls out Cicero's claim that Antony alone caused the civil war, as Helen caused the Trojan War, as plainly false (Ant. 6). He admits that Antony, so weak in good times, was at his best in misfortune; on the retreat from Mutina, this lover of luxury drank foul water and ate bark and wild roots alongside his soldiers without complaint (Ant. 17). He asks the right questions, and he answers them with stories detailed enough that we can perhaps argue back. Two thousand years later, his mirror remains rather unblemished.

The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars,
But in ourselves, that we are underlings.

— William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar
